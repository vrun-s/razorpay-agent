## Part I — The three holes that will lose you the room

### 1. The decision engine — the actual product — is undefined

Everything *around* the AI is specified: policy engine, allocator, simulator, attribution, measurement, case lifecycle. But nowhere in `CONTEXT.md` or the ADRs does it say **how the agent actually decides**.

`CONTEXT.md` defines Reassessment as "the agent re-evaluating a case's recovery probability and economics." Re-evaluating *with what*? The whole thesis is "maximize expected net recovery," which requires `P(recovered | intervention, context)` for every candidate intervention. Where does that number come from?

Three possible answers, all with consequences you haven't chosen between:

| Approach | Strength | Attack surface |
|---|---|---|
| LLM estimates probabilities directly | Fast to build, handles unstructured context | "Where does 27% come from?" → no answer. Uncalibrated. The whole EV story collapses. |
| Parametric/learned model estimates probability; LLM does context extraction + narration | Defensible numbers, calibratable | Needs training data — which only your simulator can produce → circularity (see §2) |
| Rules table + LLM only for diagnosis/explanation | Honest, robust | "So what is the AI actually doing?" (see §3) |

**This is the single most important thing to decide before writing code.** My recommendation: a hybrid — a small explicit probability model (Beta-Bernoulli priors per `(failure_reason × customer_segment_proxy × intervention)` cell, updated online from observed outcomes) as the estimator, with the LLM doing (a) failure-reason diagnosis from unstructured bank decline strings, (b) context synthesis into the model's feature cells, and (c) the natural-language reasoning trace that goes into the audit trail. That gives you a number you can defend, calibration curves you can plot, and a real role for the LLM.

Whatever you pick, **write it as ADR-0006 today**, and put a calibration plot in the demo (predicted vs. actual recovery rate, bucketed). Almost nobody at a hackathon shows a calibration curve. It is a very cheap way to look serious.

### 2. Your evaluation is circular unless you deliberately break the circle

The simulator defines ground-truth `P(recover | intervention)`. The agent estimates that same quantity. If the agent's estimator carries the simulator's assumptions — same heuristics, same feature set, same functional form, or worse, an LLM prompt informed by how you wrote the generator — then "the online agent captured 87% of offline-optimal" measures **nothing except that you wrote your assumptions down twice**.

This is the most attackable claim in the entire project and there is currently zero documented defense. Fixes, in order of value:

1. **Generate ground truth from a process the engine has no access to.** Write the simulator's response curves *first*, then build the estimator without referring to that file. If AI-assisted, use separate sessions and do not paste the generator into the estimator's context.
2. **Hold out a test set and never tune on it.** The old doc proposes 5,000 / 2,000 / 3,000 — good instinct, but it's orphaned in a deprecated document and carried into no ADR. Make it a commitment.
3. **Run a misspecification test.** Perturb the simulator's response curves (shift elasticities, change the fatigue decay, resegment the personas) and re-run. If the agent still beats baseline under a generator it wasn't shaped around, that's a real result. If it doesn't, you've learned something important *before* a judge does.
4. **Report a confidence interval.** A +6pp lift on 1,000 cases needs an interval, not a point estimate. Since you own the simulator, use the true counterfactual: replay the same case set with the same RNG seed under baseline and treatment so outcome draws are shared. That's a much tighter comparison than an A/B split, and it's a good thing to say out loud.

Add this as **ADR-0007: evaluation integrity**. Being the team that pre-emptively names its own evaluation's weakness and shows the stress test is a stronger position than being the team with the bigger number.

### 3. You have not established what the AI is actually contributing

The honest question from a Razorpay engineer: *"The policy engine bounds every action. The allocator is expected-value arithmetic. Your probabilities come from a table. What is the LLM doing that a logistic regression and a rules file couldn't?"*

Nothing in the docs answers this — and it's an **AI** Buildathon. This is not hypothetical; it's the first question I'd ask.

You need two things:

- **A stated role for the LLM** that a rules engine genuinely can't fill. Best candidates: diagnosing failure reasons from messy unstructured decline text; synthesizing a customer's narrative history into decision-relevant features; generating the human-readable justification attached to every case in the audit trail; judging escalation-worthiness where the signal is qualitative.
- **An ablation.** Run the same held-out case set three ways: rules-only, LLM-in-loop, LLM-with-history. Report net recovered for each. If LLM-in-loop wins, you have proof rather than a claim. If it doesn't — you find out privately, and you reframe the LLM as the diagnosis and explainability layer, which is still honest and still Track 3.

The ablation costs maybe half a day and is the difference between "AI project" and "project with AI in it."

## Part II — Razorpay reality problems

### 4. ⚠️ The halted-subscription workflow may be un-exercisable in 15 days

Your own research notes: *"subscriptions auto-retry 3 times over ~4 days before halting, non-interruptible by an integrator."* ADR-0001 makes halted-subscription recovery one of the two shipping workflows, on the grounds that agent authority begins at `halted`.

**If test mode does not compress that ~4-day clock, you cannot reach `halted` more than ~3 times inside the entire build window** — and not at all until Day 4 at the earliest. Combined with the noted 3-day test-mode card-token validity, engineering a subscription that authenticates, charges, fails on schedule, and halts on demand is a multi-day experiment on its own.

This is the highest-severity item in this review, because it's a load-bearing assumption sitting inside a decision you've already committed to.

**Do this on Day 1, before anything else:** create the test account, create a subscription, and find out empirically whether test mode offers a way to force or fast-forward the retry ladder to `halted`. If it doesn't, decide immediately between: (a) inject synthetic `subscription.halted` events into your own ingestion layer and state explicitly in the demo that this trigger is simulated while the *action* side is real; or (b) demote the second workflow to a stretch goal and go deep on failed-payment recovery. Either is fine. Discovering it on Day 9 is not.

### 5. The 1,000-case demo cannot run through real Razorpay

Your notes say Payment Links are capped at **30 per business in test mode**. ADR-0003's demo — and the track requirement to show "measured money recovered across a batch" — assumes ~1,000 cases. These collide, and no document reconciles them.

The resolution is straightforward, but it has to be *designed and stated*, not discovered live:

- **~20–25 cases execute end-to-end through real Razorpay test mode** (real payment link created, real test-card payment, real `payment_link.paid` webhook, real verification). This is your integration proof.
- **1,000 cases run in-simulator** for the statistics and the allocation story.
- **Say so plainly in the UI and the pitch.** A visible badge — "Razorpay test-mode execution: 23 cases · Simulated population: 1,000 cases" — converts a hidden weakness into a credibility signal. Blurring it is what actually loses trust.

Also budget the 30-link cap deliberately: you will burn links debugging. Check whether links can be cancelled/reused, and whether an Orders + hosted-checkout path gives you a less-capped execution route for some cases.

### 6. The simulator ↔ Razorpay seam needs an explicit contract

Who decides whether a customer pays? Your simulator owns the outcome distribution. Razorpay test mode owns the payment state machine. So the real loop is:

```
simulator rolls outcome → if "recovers", a test harness pays the link with a test card
→ Razorpay fires payment_link.paid → your system verifies and attributes
```

That's a legitimate design — but written down it's honest, and undocumented it looks like theater the moment a judge asks "wait, who paid that?". Put it in an ADR with exactly that diagram, and name the boundary: **outcomes are simulated; execution, state transitions, and verification are real.**

### 7. `HUMAN_ESCALATION` has no receiver

It appears in every action list. Track 3 explicitly requires "compliant escalation." Right now it's a terminal state that escalates into the void. It needs a destination: an escalation queue in the dashboard, carrying the case's full context, the reason the agent escalated, and its recommended action, with a human accept/override control that writes back to the audit trail. An override the system *records and learns from* is a strong demo beat and costs little.

### 8. Compliance is completely absent — and it's free differentiation

Track 3's bar names *compliant* escalation. India-specific constraints that touch this project directly:

- **RBI e-mandate rules** for recurring payments: pre-debit notification ahead of a debit, and additional-factor authentication above the prescribed threshold. This directly constrains what Resume Charge is allowed to do and when.
- **TRAI/DND** restrictions on commercial communication — you cannot dunning-spam a customer, which is a real-world justification for `max_interventions_per_customer` beyond "it's polite."
- **Incentive accounting** — discounts are margin and carry tax treatment; this is part of why "net recovered" is the right target.

You don't need a compliance module. **One ADR** ("recovery operates under these constraints; here is what the policy engine enforces and what it deliberately doesn't") plus two policy rules that visibly reject a non-compliant proposal in the demo would put you in a category almost nobody else is in. Verify the current rules yourself before citing specific thresholds — don't quote numbers from memory.

## Part III — Planning and process gaps

### 9. No build plan, no cut-line, no vertical slice

Components implied by current scope:

1. Webhook ingestion (HMAC verify, `x-razorpay-event-id` dedupe, out-of-order tolerance)
2. Case store + state machine + persistence
3. Scheduled sweep for response-window timeouts
4. Workflow abstraction + failed-payment workflow
5. Halted-subscription workflow
6. Context builder (Customer History + Case History)
7. Decision engine (probability estimation + EV)
8. Policy engine
9. Streaming allocator with reserve policy
10. Offline-optimal baseline calculator
11. Action executor + Razorpay client
12. Simulator (personas, state-dependent response curves, ground truth)
13. Experiment harness (baselines, metrics, incremental measurement)
14. Chaos/failure test suite
15. React dashboard + case drill-down + audit trail view

**Fifteen components, solo, fifteen days.** That's one per day with zero slack, no debugging, and no Razorpay surprises — and §4 guarantees at least one Razorpay surprise.

Suggested shape:

- **Days 1–2** — Test account + Razorpay reality checks (§4, §5). Repo skeleton, schema, one webhook end-to-end. Nothing else.
- **Days 3–6** — Vertical slice: one failed-payment case, detected → decided → policy-checked → real payment link → real webhook → verified → audited. Ugly UI is fine.
- **Day 6 gate** — If the slice isn't demoable, cut the second workflow immediately, no debate.
- **Days 7–9** — Simulator + streaming allocator + experiment harness. Produce a real number.
- **Days 10–11** — Second workflow *or* adaptive learning *or* chaos suite. Pick by what's actually working. Not all three.
- **Days 12–13** — Dashboard, audit trail view, escalation queue.
- **Days 14–15** — Ablation (§3), misspecification test (§2), demo script, recording, writeup. **Do not build here.**

**Pre-commit the cut list now, while you're not panicking:** halted-subscription workflow → adaptive learning → chaos beyond 3 scenarios → conversational recovery. Cut in that order. A tight failed-payment system with honest measurement beats two half-broken workflows, every time.

### 10. No spec, no tickets — despite the repo declaring an issue tracker

`CLAUDE.md` and `docs/agents/issue-tracker.md` define a `.scratch/<feature-slug>/` convention. `.scratch/` doesn't exist. You have decisions but no work items — exactly the state where a solo builder drifts. Write `.scratch/recovery-engine/spec.md` and the first 8–10 issue files before writing code.

### 11. Document drift is already live

`docs/razorpay_track3_initial_context.md` is 1,255 lines and now partly wrong. The status header helps, but:

- "Tier S must-have **#1**: Predictive revenue-risk detection" — explicitly deprioritized in the status block
- "Strongest demo concept" assumes batch allocation — superseded by ADR-0003
- The 5,000/2,000/3,000 dataset split is a *good* idea that lives only in the deprecated doc and appears in no ADR

Any agent — or you, on Day 11, tired — reading the wrong section builds the wrong thing. Either prune it to a short "ideas considered and rejected" appendix, or mark superseded sections inline with `> SUPERSEDED by ADR-000X`. Promote the dataset split into an ADR before you lose it.

### 12. `.gitignore` bug

`*.json` is unqualified — it will silently exclude `package.json`, `tsconfig.json`, `skills-lock.json`, and every test fixture you write. Narrow it to the files you actually meant.

### 13. The demo isn't designed

Your strongest ideas — the reserve budget held back for cases that haven't arrived, a case reassessed three times and then deliberately stopped, `NO_ACTION` chosen on purpose — are all **invisible** unless the UI is built to show them. The "Revenue Recovery Inbox" sketch shows totals, which is the boring part.

What a judge should see in 3–5 minutes:

1. A live case timeline: detected → decision + reasoning → policy check (naming the constraint that bound it) → execution → webhook → reassessment → stop. With timestamps.
2. The budget reserve as a moving quantity, with one visible moment where the allocator declines a mediocre case and later spends on a better one.
3. One `NO_ACTION` case that recovered anyway — proof you didn't waste money.
4. One policy rejection, and one escalation with a human override.
5. The aggregate: net recovered vs. two baselines, with an interval, plus % of offline-optimal.
6. The calibration curve.

Write the demo script on Day 3, not Day 14. It changes what you build.

## Part IV — What's genuinely strong (keep these; they're your edge)

- **`CONTEXT.md`'s ubiquitous language.** The `_Avoid_` annotations and the Payment Retry / Resume Charge split are real domain modeling. Most submissions have no vocabulary discipline at all.
- **ADR-0001.** You killed your own originating idea for a documented API-availability reason. That reads as engineering maturity, not indecision. Mention it in the pitch.
- **ADR-0003.** The best decision in the set. Refusing batch optimization because "the agent can't know about case #999 while deciding case #1" — then still computing offline-optimal as an *evaluation baseline* — is exactly right, and it's the kind of thing that makes a payments engineer sit up.
- **ADR-0005.** "Silence is itself a trigger" is a genuine insight and maps directly to the track's promise-to-pay example.
- **Persona as hidden ground truth.** Forcing the engine to infer customer type from observable history rather than being handed the label is the correct discipline, and most people get this wrong.
- **The Razorpay-specific research** (retry ladder, link caps, webhook ordering/dedupe, HMAC over the raw body, token validity). This is what separates a Razorpay project from a Stripe project with the logo swapped.

## Bottom line

You have done the hard *thinking*. The decisions are better than most of what will be submitted. But right now you have a well-argued position paper and zero running code on Day 0 of 15, with a load-bearing Razorpay assumption unverified (§4), the core decision mechanism unspecified (§1), and an evaluation design that a sharp judge can dismantle with one question (§2).

Fix those three, cut the scope by a third, and have the vertical slice running by Day 6. A narrow system that measures itself honestly and knows exactly where its own numbers are soft will beat a broad system with impressive numbers nobody can defend — especially in front of payments engineers, who are professionally trained to distrust a number without an error bar.

The riskiest thing you can do now is spend Day 1 writing more planning documents. Go create the test account.
